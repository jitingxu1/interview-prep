# Roblox Critical Harm Context

## What You Need to Know

### Roblox Platform Basics
- **Scale**: 220M+ monthly active users (mostly under 16)
- **Core**: User-generated content platform—creators build games/experiences
- **Revenue**: In-app purchases (Robux currency)
- **Risk**: Child safety, moderation, protection of young users

### Critical Harm Domain
**Critical harm** typically means:
- **Child safety**: Protecting minors from predation, inappropriate content, exploitation
- **Toxicity & harassment**: Bullying, hate speech, doxxing
- **Scams & fraud**: Robux scams, phishing, account takeovers (especially kids' accounts)
- **Privacy violations**: Inappropriate data collection or sharing
- **Adult content**: Exposure of minors to mature/explicit content

Critical harm ≠ regular moderation. It's the intersection of:
1. Real harm to users (especially children)
2. Platform responsibility (duty of care)
3. Legal/regulatory risk (COPPA, UK Online Safety Bill, etc.)

## Why Your Fraud Detection Experience Matters

### Parallel Thinking
Your fraud detection work at DoorDash has trained you for critical harm:

| Fraud Detection | Critical Harm |
|---|---|
| "How do we catch bad actors without blocking good users?" | "How do we protect kids without making the platform unusable?" |
| High false positive = user friction | High false positive = blocking legitimate creators/players |
| High false negative = fraud losses | High false negative = harm to vulnerable users |
| Adversarial: fraud patterns evolve | Adversarial: bad actors evolve tactics |
| Cross-functional (ops, legal, business) | Cross-functional (legal, policy, trust & safety, product) |
| Ambiguous thresholds (how much fraud is acceptable?) | Ambiguous thresholds (how much risk is acceptable?) |

### Your Competitive Advantage
1. **You understand tradeoff thinking**: You've had to balance false positives vs. false negatives in a high-stakes domain. Same skill applies to critical harm.
2. **You're used to regulatory/legal pressure**: Fraud touches compliance; critical harm definitely does (COPPA, international laws).
3. **You know how to work cross-functionally**: You've aligned with business, ops, and legal. Critical harm requires that.
4. **You're comfortable with ambiguity**: There's no perfect fraud model; there's no perfect child safety system. You make the best call you can.

## How to Position Yourself

### Opening Statement (if asked "Why critical harm?")
**"I've spent the last year in fraud detection—a domain where you have to balance catching bad actors with not harming legitimate users. That's taught me to think deeply about tradeoffs, safety, and user impact. Critical harm is the natural next step for me because the stakes are even higher—you're protecting vulnerable users. The skills transfer directly: understanding adversarial thinking, working cross-functionally with legal/policy, and making sound decisions under uncertainty. I want to build systems that keep kids safe while letting creators thrive."**

### In Technical Discussions
- Draw parallels to fraud detection without forced analogies
- Show you understand the regulatory/legal angle (not just ML)
- Emphasize user empathy and safety-first thinking
- Ask thoughtful questions about their ML approach and challenges

### In Manager-Level Discussions
- How do you motivate a team working on hard, often-thankless problems?
- How do you measure success in critical harm? (Metrics are harder than fraud $$)
- How do you scale a team from reactive moderation to proactive prevention?
- How do you build psychological safety when discussing sensitive topics (abuse, exploitation)?

## Talking Points to Weave In

1. **"I care about safety"** — Fraud detection taught you that user trust is foundational. Same for critical harm.
2. **"I understand regulations matter"** — You've worked with legal and compliance. Critical harm has COPPA, GDPR, LGPD, etc.
3. **"I think about unintended consequences"** — Stricter policies can reduce harms but also block legitimate activity. You know how to navigate that.
4. **"I'm a learner on the safety/policy side"** — You can be honest that while you know ML and scaling teams, the specific regulatory landscape around child safety is new. That's OK—you're eager to learn and bring your core skills.
5. **"I've worked on problems where perfect isn't possible"** — Critical harm is like that. You make the best decision you can and iterate.

---

## Red Flags to Avoid

- Don't position this as "pivoting away from fraud because I'm bored"
- Don't oversell your knowledge of child safety (you don't have it—yet)
- Don't make it sound like critical harm = risk aversion (it's about smart, proportionate decisions)
- Don't downplay the emotional weight of the work (you're protecting kids; it's serious)
