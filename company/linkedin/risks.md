# Honest Gaps — And What to Patch in One Day

An honest read of your prep against what a leadership screen actually probes. Fix the first two. The rest, just know they're there.

---

## 1. The "why leave" answer will hurt you — **fix today**

Covered fully in `why_linkedin.md`. The current framing ("I want a startup, less legacy, less org burden") argues against joining LinkedIn. This is the one thing that can end the screen on its own.

**Patch**: read that file, say the answer aloud until it's natural. 20 minutes.

---

## 2. You have no failure-of-execution story — **fix today**

Look at your ten stories: every one resolves well. Zero attrition, shipped four weeks early, promoted, recovered, greenlit. A senior interviewer notices this pattern immediately, and the read is either *this person hasn't operated at real stakes* or *this person can't see their own misses*.

S5 is your only miss, and it's a people-feedback miss, not an execution one. "Tell me about a project that slipped" is asked in most manager screens.

**Patch**: spend 20 minutes finding one real example. A deadline you missed, a model that underperformed in production, a project you had to kill, a bet that didn't pay off. Four bullets is enough:
- What you committed to and what actually happened
- When you first knew, and what you did that day
- How you told the stakeholders (this is the part they're grading)
- What you changed structurally afterward

You don't need it polished. You need to not be caught empty.

---

## 3. Your numbers are thin

Recurring figures across your stories: "40% of fraud, <2% false positives," "12 engineers," "50% → 20% meetings." That's three numbers carrying ten stories, and none of them are in dollars.

At a leadership screen, "we reduced fraud" gets "by how much?" within one beat.

**Patch**: write down three real numbers on a sticky note — fraud loss prevented in dollars or percent, team size grown from → to, and one scale number (requests/day, deliveries scored, model latency). If you can't share exact figures, practice the graceful version: *"I can't give exact numbers, but the order of magnitude was —."* That's fine. Silence isn't.

---

## 4. "Influence at scale" is your weakest of the four themes

The recruiter named it explicitly. Your best example (S10) is one partner team over one initiative. At LinkedIn, "at scale" means many teams, many layers, and often influencing without ever being in the room.

**Don't overclaim.** Tell S10 as your concrete example, then generalize the playbook — *find out whether their no is about their metric or their capacity, then absorb some of that cost yourself.* Frame the larger scale as what you're moving toward. That's more credible than inflating one story.

---

## 5. Manager-of-managers is unproven

You lead 12 across two pods with senior ICs owning areas. That's a strong single-team manager, not a manager of managers. If this LinkedIn role is the latter, don't pretend.

**The honest framing**: "I've run two pods with senior ICs owning technical direction in each, which is the closest thing I've done. I haven't managed managers, and I'd want to be clear about that. What I have done is build a team from zero and grow people into ownership — and my read is that's the harder half of the skill."

---

## 6. Several stories read as constructed

"Alex told me later: *I needed someone to believe in me before I believed in myself*" (S7) and the parallel line in S9 are too tidy to be quoted verbatim. Experienced interviewers hear polish and start discounting. Use those stories, but drop the closing quotes and keep the specifics instead — what the growth plan contained, what feedback you gave that stung.

---

## 7. Second-person voice

Most STAR files are written as "You called a team meeting." If any of that phrasing survives into your speech, it sounds coached. `story_cuts.md` has the four you're most likely to need in first person. For the rest, just remember to say "I."

---

## What you're strong on — don't over-prepare these

- **Building from scratch.** 12 engineers from zero is a genuinely strong headline. Say it in the first two minutes.
- **S8 (right person, wrong seat).** Most candidates only have PIP stories. This one shows diagnosis and it's memorable.
- **S10 (cross-org).** Rebuilding a partner team's system to earn their yes is a real, specific, senior move.
- **Technical credibility.** Ibis and Ibis-ML are real open-source projects with real adoption. That's verifiable substance most EM candidates don't have.
