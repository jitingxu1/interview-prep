# Why LinkedIn / Why Leaving DoorDash

**This is the highest-risk answer in your kit. Read this before anything else.**

---

## The problem with your current answer

`self-introduction/why_leave_doordash_long.md` says, in substance:

> "Thin margins. There's a ceiling. I want a **startup** — less legacy code, less organizational burden, more room to innovate. I want something that feels like it's just getting started."

LinkedIn is a 20+ year old company with roughly 20,000 employees, owned by Microsoft, running enormous legacy infrastructure. If you give that answer tomorrow, Sergei hears: *this person will be unhappy here in eight months.* It's the single fastest way to lose this screen.

The instinct underneath it is real and fine. The framing is wrong. Fix the framing, keep the truth.

---

## The reframe

Your actual complaint isn't "big company." It's that **at DoorDash, ML is a cost lever on a low-margin logistics business.** A better fraud model saves money. It doesn't change what the product can do for a person. The ceiling you're feeling is economic, not organizational.

That reframe travels well, because the contrast with LinkedIn is genuine: at LinkedIn, the matching problem *is* the product. A better model doesn't shave cost — it changes whether someone finds a job.

Lead with the pull, not the push.

---

## The answer (60–75 seconds, say it out loud until it's yours)

> "I've had a good run at DoorDash — I built the ML and AI team from nothing, and the work is real. But I've gotten clear about what I want next.
>
> At DoorDash, machine learning is essentially an efficiency lever on a logistics business with thin margins. A better fraud model saves the company money. That's valuable, and I've been good at it. But it doesn't change what the product does for anyone. After a few years of that, I want the model to *be* the product.
>
> That's what pulls me toward LinkedIn. The core problem — connecting people to opportunity, at the scale of the whole professional world — is fundamentally a matching and ranking problem. A better model there means someone actually finds a job. That's a different kind of ceiling.
>
> And selfishly, the problems line up with what I've spent my career on. At Airbnb I worked on getting the right content to the right user at the right time. At DoorDash I've built fraud and abuse detection and agentic systems for support automation. Those are the same problem shapes as relevance, trust, and AI-assisted product surfaces here — just at a scale I haven't worked at before. That's the part I want."

---

## If he pushes: "You told the recruiter you were looking at startups"

> "What I meant was scope, not size. I want to be on a team where the charter is still being defined rather than maintained — where I'm building the thing, not operating someone else's. My read is that AI at LinkedIn is exactly that right now: it's an expanding mandate inside a company that already has the data and the reach. A startup gives you the greenfield but not the scale. I'd rather have both."

Then stop talking. Don't over-defend it.

---

## If he asks: "What would make you leave us in two years?"

Be honest and specific — this is a trust question, not a trap.

> "If I stopped learning, or if I were maintaining something rather than building it. I'm not motivated by scope for its own sake — I'm motivated by hard problems where the technical work actually matters to the outcome. As long as that's true, I stay."

---

## Words to keep out of your mouth tomorrow

| Don't say | Because |
|---|---|
| "startup" | LinkedIn is not one. It reads as a countdown clock. |
| "legacy code" / "legacy infrastructure" | LinkedIn has more of it than DoorDash. |
| "organizational burden" / "too much process" | Reads as someone who'll fight the org. |
| "thin margins" as the headline | Makes it about your last employer, not about LinkedIn. Use it only as one clause inside the larger point about ML being a cost lever. |
| "less bureaucracy" | Same problem. |
| "I want more upside" | Reads as compensation-driven. |

The rule: **make it about the problem, not about the company you're leaving.**

## The honest version of "why DoorDash was good" (have this ready too)

Interviewers get suspicious when a candidate has nothing good to say. Bank two sentences:

> "DoorDash is where I became a manager. I built a 12-person team from zero, shipped fraud systems that actually held up, and learned how to lead through a transition where I had no playbook. I'd do it again."

Then pivot to the pull.
