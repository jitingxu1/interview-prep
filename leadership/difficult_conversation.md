# "Tell me about a difficult conversation you had. How did you prepare?"

Full spoken answer, prep notes, checklist, follow-ups, and practice log. Built from mock practice on Nov 23. Company-specific version: [Rula / Jay Floyd Q10](../company/rula/jay-floyd-leadership-qa.md).

**This is the home for the "tech lead's manager path was cancelled" story.** If a "bad news from above" question comes up later (e.g. "how do you communicate bad news to the team?"), refer back to it in one line. The older blindsided-engineer story ([weaknesses.md](./weaknesses.md) #3, [building_team_and_culture.md](./building_team_and_culture.md)) is the backup for this question.

---

## Answer structure

| Step | What to say |
|---|---|
| **1. Headline** | One sentence: what the conversation was and why it was hard |
| **2. Context** | The plan, and what changed |
| **3. How I prepared** | Facts, what I could honestly offer, how they'd feel, timing |
| **4. The conversation** | The news in the first minute, acknowledge the feeling, the honest picture, the plan |
| **5. Result** | What you actually saw afterward |
| **6. What I learned** | The system-level lesson |

---

## Final answer (~2 minutes)

> The hardest conversation I've had was earlier this year, with my tech lead. For months we'd been preparing him to become an engineering manager. Then an org change took that path away, and I had to tell him.
>
> **Context:** he wanted to move into management, and we'd built a real plan together: the skills, feedback from leadership, the calibration. It was going well. Then midyear, with **[a hiring freeze / reorg]**, the company set a new rule: every manager needs at least 10 to 15 reports. Our team couldn't support a second manager, so the promotion was off the table.
>
> **How I prepared:** first, I got the facts straight from leadership: what was final, and what might change. Second, I decided what I could honestly offer, and what I couldn't promise. Third, I thought about how he'd feel. He'd done everything right, so I knew this would feel unfair. And I didn't wait: I told him within **[a day or two]** of hearing, so he wouldn't hear it from anyone else.
>
> **The conversation:** I said it in the first minute, clearly: the manager role isn't going to happen right now, and here's why. He was disappointed and asked, "What happens now?" I didn't rush past that. I acknowledged it was unfair given the work he'd put in. Then I gave him the honest picture and a plan: he'd keep growing as a manager without the title. He now leads a sub-team of the fraud ML team, mentors them, and does most of a manager's job. I was clear that I couldn't promise a date, only that if the team grows, he's the first person I'll put forward.
>
> **Result:** it took a month or two, but he came back fully engaged. **[REAL RESULT: e.g. he stayed, the sub-team shipped X, or he's now leading Y.]**
>
> **What I learned:** don't tie someone's growth plan to a title or a date you don't control. Now I build growth plans around readiness and scope, so a change in the org doesn't erase the progress.

**[CONFIRM BEFORE USING LIVE]**
- **What caused the change:** a hiring freeze, a reorg, or the new span-of-control rule on its own? (The practice transcript said "higher speeds," which is probably a dictation error.)
- **"Calibration" vs. "certifications":** which did you actually mean?
- **How fast you told him:** the same day? within a few days?
- **The real result:** did he stay, and what is he doing now?
- **The careful promise:** did you actually say "no date, but if the team grows you're first"? Only say it if you did.

**Why this lands:**
- Recent, high-stakes, and the bad news came from above.
- "How did you prepare?" gets a clear answer: facts, what to offer, their feelings, timing.
- The news comes in the first minute; the feeling is acknowledged; the promise is careful.
- The lesson is at the system level: you changed how you set up growth plans, not just this one case.

For a coaching-heavy interviewer like Rula's Jay Floyd, it covers his "hard conversations" step (structure and timing) and "calm authority" (bad news from above).

## Prep notes: what "readiness and scope" means

The lesson line changes **what a growth plan is aimed at**.

| | Tied to a title and a date | Tied to readiness and scope |
|---|---|---|
| **The goal** | "You'll be an engineering manager by Q3." | "You'll be ready to manage: leading a sub-team, running 1:1s, handling stakeholders, hiring." |
| **What progress means** | Getting the title | Growing skills and real responsibility, step by step |
| **If the org changes** | The plan looks like a failure, and the progress feels erased | The skills and scope stay with the person; only the title waits |

- **Readiness:** the skills and behaviors of the next level. Can they actually do the job?
- **Scope:** the real responsibility they hold: how many people, which areas, which decisions.

Both belong to the person, and they don't disappear when the org changes. Your tech lead kept the skills he built and still leads a sub-team; only the title was delayed. This matches a common promotion principle (and Jay's): recognise work already being done, don't promise future work.

**Simpler version of the lesson, if you need it:**
> "What I learned is not to promise a title or a date I don't control. Now I set growth goals around skills and responsibility, things the person keeps even if the org changes. The title comes when the role exists."

## Checklist before you speak

- [ ] Headline in the first sentence
- [ ] Tell it in order: context → preparation → conversation → result → lesson
- [ ] Answer "how did you prepare?" explicitly, with three things plus timing
- [ ] The news in the first minute, no long wind-up
- [ ] Acknowledge the feeling before the plan
- [ ] A careful promise only: no date
- [ ] One real result
- [ ] Close with the readiness-and-scope lesson

## Likely follow-ups

### Quick hints

| Follow-up | Hint | Key line |
|---|---|---|
| What did you say in the first minute? | **News first, then why** | "The manager role isn't going to happen right now, and here's why." |
| Could you have seen it coming? | **Own the date, not the org** | "I tied his plan to a title and a timeline I didn't control." |
| How did you keep him from leaving? | **Real scope, honest odds, regular check-ins** | "He kept the job, just not the title." |
| How did you handle the rest of the team? | **His news first, his to share** | "He heard it from me first, and how it was shared was up to him." |

### 1. What did you say in the first minute?

> Something like: "I have some hard news, and I want to be direct. The manager role we've been working toward isn't going to happen right now. The company has a new rule that every manager needs at least 10 to 15 reports, and our team can't support a second one. I know how much work you've put in, and I'm sorry." Then I stopped and let him react, before getting into the plan.

### 2. Could you have seen it coming, or set expectations differently?

> I couldn't have predicted the org change. But I could have framed the plan differently. I'd tied it to a title and a timeline, which I didn't control. If I'd framed it around readiness and scope from the start, the news would still have hurt, but it wouldn't have felt like all the progress was gone. That's what I changed afterward.

### 3. How did you keep him from leaving?

> By making sure the growth was real, not a consolation prize. He leads a sub-team, runs their 1:1s, and handles their stakeholders, so he's building the same skills he would as a manager. I was honest about the odds, with no fake dates. And I checked in more often for the first couple of months, because disappointment doesn't go away after one conversation.

**[CONFIRM: is he still on the team, and did you increase check-ins?]**

### 4. How did you handle it with the rest of the team?

> He heard it from me first, before anyone else. Then I asked how he wanted it shared. Because he'd been leading the sub-team informally, I made his role explicit to the team: he leads that sub-team and runs their day-to-day. That way the team saw his scope grow, not shrink.

**[CONFIRM: did the team know about the planned promotion, and how was it shared?]**
