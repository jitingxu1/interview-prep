# "How do you approach leveling and promotions? How do you keep it fair?"

A primer on how leveling works, the spoken answer, two deep-dive follow-ups, and a third with hints. Built from mock practice on Nov 24. Company-specific version: [Rula / Jay Floyd Q19](../company/rula/jay-floyd-leadership-qa.md).

---

## Primer: how leveling and promotions work at most tech companies

**Levels** are steps on a career ladder, e.g. Engineer I → II → Senior → Staff → Principal. Each level has written expectations, usually across a few dimensions:

| Dimension | What changes as you go up |
|---|---|
| **Scope** | Task → feature → system → multiple teams → org |
| **Technical depth** | Follows designs → designs systems → sets technical direction |
| **Independence** | Needs guidance → works alone → guides others |
| **Influence** | Own work → helps teammates → shapes other teams' decisions |
| **Impact** | Delivers tasks → delivers projects → moves business metrics |

**A promotion** usually means someone has been **consistently working at the next level for a while** (often 6+ months), not that they've been there long or tried hard.

**The process** at most companies:
1. **The manager writes a promotion case** with evidence: projects, scope, impact, peer feedback.
2. **Calibration:** a group of managers reviews the cases together, so one generous manager doesn't promote faster than a strict one.
3. **A committee or leadership approves it.**

### What makes it unfair, and the fix for each

| Unfairness | Fix |
|---|---|
| Expectations live only in the manager's head | Share the written level guide; tell each person exactly what the next level looks like for them |
| Loud people get noticed; quiet ones don't | Judge by evidence (work, outcomes, peer feedback), not visibility in meetings |
| Some people get the high-profile projects, others don't | Rotate stretch work fairly; track who gets what |
| Promoting for effort, tenure, or as a retention deal | Promote only on demonstrated next-level work |
| Recency: one great month or one bad week | Keep notes across the whole cycle |
| One manager's taste | Calibration across managers; peer input |
| Surprises at review time | Talk about level and gaps in 1:1s all year |

---

## Answer structure: transparent → plan → evidence → calibrate

| Step | What you say |
|---|---|
| **1. Transparent** | Everyone knows what's expected at their level and the next one, in specific terms |
| **2. Plan** | A growth plan to the next level, built on their strengths; stretch work that proves it |
| **3. Evidence** | Promote on work already being done, consistently, not on promises |
| **4. Calibrate** | Fairness checks: written rubric, peer input, notes across the cycle, fair access to stretch work, calibration across managers |

---

## Final answer (~90 seconds)

> I keep it fair by making it transparent and evidence-based.
>
> **Transparent:** every person on my team knows what's expected at their level and the next one, in specific terms. People are most frustrated not when they hear "not yet," but when there's no clear bar. "You did great": great at what? So I translate the level guide into what it means for their work.
>
> **Plan:** if they want the next level, we build a plan around their strengths. Two people can reach the same level in different ways: one through technical depth, another through leading projects. Then I find stretch work that proves it. I build these plans around readiness and scope, not a date, because I can't promise a date.
>
> **Evidence:** I only put someone forward when they're already working at the next level consistently. The promotion should recognise work that's already happening, not a promise about the future. If I've done my job, the case writes itself.
>
> **Fairness checks:** I judge on evidence, not on who's loudest in meetings. I get peer input, I keep notes across the whole cycle so it's not just the last month, and I rotate high-visibility work so everyone gets a chance to show it. Then calibration with other managers keeps my bar consistent with theirs.

## Likely follow-ups

### Quick hints

| Follow-up | Hint | Key line |
|---|---|---|
| They think they deserve it, you don't? | **Listen → bar, not peers → specific gap → plan → monthly check-in** | "Not 'you're not ready,' but here's exactly what the next level looks like." |
| Rejected in calibration? | **Understand → own it → tell them honestly → rebuild → calibrate earlier** | "Usually part of it is on me. The case should have been undeniable." |
| Quiet engineers overlooked? | **Evidence, not airtime; give them the stage; speak for them in calibration** | "My job is to make their work visible, not to make them louder." |

### 1. What do you do when someone thinks they deserve a promotion and you don't?

**What the interviewer is testing:** whether you can say "not yet" honestly, with evidence, without crushing motivation or caving in.

**Framework: listen → compare to the bar → name the gap → plan → check in**

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| **1. Listen first** | Ask for their case: "Walk me through why you think you're ready." Sometimes they know about work you missed. |
| **2. Compare to the bar, not to people** | Put their work next to the written next-level expectations. Talk about dimensions (scope, independence, influence), not about a peer. |
| **3. Name the gap specifically** | Not "you're not ready," but "the next level means leading a cross-team project end to end. So far you've led within the team." |
| **4. Agree on a plan** | One or two concrete things that would close the gap, plus stretch work that shows it. |
| **5. Check in regularly** | Monthly, against the plan. When they hit it, you put them forward. |

**Why people think they're ready, and what each needs:**

| What's behind it | What to do |
|---|---|
| **They've been here a long time** | Explain it's about next-level work, not tenure. Show what that work looks like. |
| **A peer got promoted** | Don't discuss the peer. Talk about their own work against the bar. |
| **They're doing great work at their current level** | Acknowledge it. "Great at your level" and "ready for the next" are different, and both are true. |
| **You're actually missing something** | Check it. If they're right, say so and update your view. |
| **The bar was never clear** | That's on you. Make it clear now, and don't count the unclear time against them. |

**Phrases:**
- "Help me see what I might be missing."
- "You're doing strong work at your level. The next level is a different kind of work. Here's what it looks like."
- "Here are the two things that would make the case. Let's find a project that shows them."
- "I want to be the one who puts your case forward. Let's build it together."

**Traps:** a vague "not yet" with no specific gap · comparing to a named peer · promising a date you don't control · promoting them to avoid the conversation or keep them (it sets them up to fail at the next level).

**Draft answer (~60 seconds):**
> First I listen. I ask them to walk me through their case, because sometimes they know about work I've missed, and if so I change my view. Then I compare their work to the written expectations for the next level, not to anyone else on the team. And I name the gap specifically. Not "you're not ready," but "the next level means leading a cross-team project end to end, and so far you've led within the team." Then we agree on one or two things that would close the gap, and I find a project that lets them show it. We check in monthly. What I won't do is promise a date, because I can't control that. But I tell them I want to be the one who puts their case forward, and we build it together.

### 2. How do you handle a promotion that gets rejected in calibration?

**What the interviewer is testing:** ownership (do you blame the committee?), honesty with the engineer, and whether you learn from it.

**Framework: understand → own it → tell them → rebuild the case → learn**

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| **1. Understand the "why"** | Get the specific feedback from calibration: missing evidence, scope not at the bar, unclear impact, or no peer support? |
| **2. Own your part** | Usually it's partly on the manager: a weak case, not enough evidence, or not calibrated with other managers beforehand. |
| **3. Tell them quickly and honestly** | Soon after, in person. Share the real reason, not "the committee didn't agree." Don't blame the committee. |
| **4. Rebuild the case** | Turn the feedback into a plan: the specific gap, the stretch work, and a check-in before the next cycle. |
| **5. Fix how you prepare cases** | Calibrate earlier: share drafts with peer managers, get peer feedback in advance, document impact during the cycle. |

**Common reasons cases get rejected, and the fix:**

| Reason | Fix next time |
|---|---|
| **Not enough evidence** | Keep notes all cycle; collect concrete examples and peer feedback early |
| **Scope isn't at the next level** | Make sure the stretch work really is next-level, not just more work |
| **Impact isn't clear** | Write impact in business terms: dollars, customers, risk |
| **Other managers don't know the person** | Give them visibility earlier: demos, cross-team work, design reviews |
| **Your bar was more generous than the org's** | Calibrate with peer managers *before* submitting |

**Phrases:**
- To the engineer: "The case didn't get through. Here's the real feedback. Part of it is on me: I didn't make the evidence clear enough. Here's what we'll do before next cycle."
- To yourself: "What would have made this case undeniable?"

**Traps:** blaming the committee ("they just didn't get it" makes the process look unfair) · hiding or softening the reason · promising next cycle for sure · not learning anything, so the same weak case gets rejected again.

**Draft answer (~60 seconds):**
> First I get the specific reason from calibration, whether it was evidence, scope, or impact. Then I look at my own part, because usually some of it is on me: the case wasn't strong enough, or I hadn't checked my bar with other managers beforehand. I tell the engineer quickly and honestly, with the real feedback, not "the committee didn't agree." And I own my part in front of them. Then we turn the feedback into a plan for the next cycle: the specific gap, the stretch work that closes it, and check-ins along the way. And I change how I prepare cases: I share drafts with peer managers earlier and collect evidence all cycle, so the next case is undeniable.

**[OPTIONAL STORY: a real case that was rejected or delayed. If you don't have one, say so: "It hasn't happened to me yet, but here's how I'd handle it." Don't invent one.]**

Same principle as [difficult_conversation.md](./difficult_conversation.md): bad news quickly and honestly, a careful promise, and a plan.

### 3. How do you make sure quiet engineers don't get overlooked?

> I judge on evidence, not airtime: design docs, code reviews, outcomes, and what their peers say about working with them. Quiet people often do the work that holds everything together, like reviews, mentoring, and fixing what nobody else wants to touch, so I make that work visible. I give them a stage that suits them: a written design doc or a small-group demo, not necessarily a big meeting. And in calibration, I'm their advocate: I bring the specific evidence, so their case doesn't depend on whether other managers have heard them speak. My job is to make their work visible, not to make them louder.
