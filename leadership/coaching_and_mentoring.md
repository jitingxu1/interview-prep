## Final answer (~90 seconds)

> I coach by starting with where each person wants to go, then building a plan around their strengths. And I know it's working when the way they make decisions changes.
>
> **Understand:** in our first career conversation, I ask where they want to be in a year or two, and what kind of work gives them energy, not just what they're good at. I also get input from their peers and watch their work myself, so I have more than one view of their strengths and gaps.
>
> **Plan:** we pick one or two areas to grow, not ten. Then I find stretch work that builds exactly those areas. How much direction I give depends on how new the skill is to them, as I described earlier.
>
> **Rhythm:** our 1:1s are mostly about growth, not status. I ask more than I tell, feedback comes within a week, and once a month we check in on the bigger goal.
>
> **[EXAMPLE: one engineer: their goal, the stretch work, the result. E.g. "One engineer wanted to grow into technical leadership. I gave them ownership of the design for [X], and coached them through the first review. Six months later they were running design reviews for the team."]**
>
> **How I know it's working:** three signals. They bring me decisions they've already made, instead of questions. They give each other feedback directly, instead of routing it through me. And they disagree with me in a meeting, with data. When I see those, they've grown, and they don't need me for that anymore.

### 1. How do you coach someone who doesn't know what they want?

**Framework: explore → experiment → reflect**

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| **Explore** | Ask about energy, not titles: "What was your best week here? Your worst? What do you wish you did more of?" Look back at what they're proudest of. |
| **Offer options** | Name the common paths so they're not choosing from a blank page: technical depth, technical leadership, people management, product or domain expertise. |
| **Experiment** | Small, low-risk tries: lead one design review, mentor an intern, own one stakeholder relationship, go deep on one hard problem. |
| **Reflect** | After each experiment: "What gave you energy? What drained you?" Repeat every month or two. |

> That's really common, and I don't push for a big answer. I start with energy, not titles: what was your best week here, and what do you wish you did more of? Then I name the usual paths, like going deeper technically, leading technically, managing people, or becoming the domain expert, so they're not choosing from a blank page. Then we try small experiments: lead one design review, mentor a new hire, own one stakeholder. After each one we talk about what gave them energy. After a few rounds, the direction usually becomes clear on its own. You don't need a five-year plan; you need the next experiment.

### 2. What do you do when someone's goal doesn't fit what the team needs?

**Framework: be honest → find the overlap → build a bridge → or help them move**

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| **Be honest** | Say clearly what the team needs and what it can't offer right now. No vague hope. |
| **Find the overlap** | Most goals have a part that also helps the team. Find that part. |
| **Build a bridge** | Give them a slice of the goal: a time-boxed project, part of their time, an adjacent responsibility. |
| **Or help them move** | If there's truly no overlap, help them find a team where the goal fits. Keeping them stuck costs everyone. |

> First I'm honest about what the team needs and what it can't offer right now, because vague hope is worse than a clear no. Then I look for the overlap. Most goals have some part that also helps the team. If someone wants to move toward product, for example, they can own the stakeholder side of one project. If there's a real gap, I build a bridge: a time-boxed project or part of their time on the thing they want to grow. And if there's truly no fit, I help them find a team where there is one. I'd rather help someone grow out of my team than keep them stuck in it. People remember that, and it builds trust with everyone else too.

### 3. How do you mentor senior engineers, who may know more than you?

**Framework: don't compete on depth; add value where they can't see**

| What you add | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Context** | Business priorities, what leadership is worried about, where the org is heading |
| **Questions, not answers** | "What's the risk if this is wrong?" "Who else should weigh in?" |
| **Scope and influence** | Push them beyond their own code: technical strategy, multiplying others, cross-team decisions (the gap between senior and staff) |
| **Visibility and access** | Put them in rooms with leadership, give them credit in public, build their promotion case |
| **Feedback on impact** | Not on their code, but on how their work lands: communication, influence, bringing others along |
| **Other mentors** | Connect them to a staff engineer or another expert for technical depth you don't have |

> I don't try to compete on technical depth. Often they know more than I do in their area, and that's good. I add value where they can't see from their seat. I give them context: business priorities, what leadership worries about. I ask questions, like "what's the risk if this is wrong?" or "who else needs to be in this decision?" And I push their scope beyond their own code, toward technical strategy and making others better, which is usually the gap to the next level. I also open doors: I put them in rooms with leadership and give them credit in public. When they need deep technical mentoring, I connect them with a staff engineer. My job isn't to know more than them; it's to help them have more impact.
