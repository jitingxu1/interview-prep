# Q10a. How do you say no to a request from someone senior that you think is wrong?

[← Round 2 index](README.md)

This is the hardest political question in the round, and it often arrives as a Q10 follow-up instead of a standalone question. It tests two failure modes at once: the manager who says yes to everything above them (a relay), and the one who blocks and becomes known as difficult (a fiefdom). What scores is a no that leaves the senior person better informed and still glad they asked you.

**The script:**

> First I assume I'm missing context, because I usually am. A senior person asking for something that looks wrong from my seat often knows about a customer commitment, a board conversation, or a reorg I don't. So the first conversation is a question, not a pushback: "Help me understand what this needs to achieve — what happens if we don't do it?" Half the time the answer changes my mind, and then it's an easy yes.
>
> If I still think it's wrong, I almost never say a flat no. I separate the goal from the solution they specified. The goal is usually right; the solution is where the problem is. So I come back with options: "Here's what it would take to do exactly this — and here's what it pushes out. Here's a version that gets you about 80% of the outcome in a third of the time. Here's what I'd recommend, and why." That turns "no" into a choice they get to make with real information, which is what a senior person actually wants from me.
>
> I make the cost concrete and name the people affected — "this delays the risk-scoring migration two months, and the Payments team is planning around that date" — not "we're at capacity." Everyone is at capacity.
>
> On the politics: if the request skips my manager, I tell my manager before I respond, so they're never surprised and I'm never caught between two people. I put my recommendation in writing, so the tradeoff is on record and doesn't depend on who remembers the meeting. And I don't escalate around the person — if I need a broader decision, I ask them to take it up together: "This is bigger than the two of us; should we bring it to X together?"
>
> And if they hear all of it and still want the original thing, I do it, properly. I've said my piece once, clearly, in writing. After that, relitigating it or doing it half-heartedly is worse than either outcome. The way I protect my credibility for the next no is by being visibly committed after this one.

**The sentence that does the work:** "Here's what it would take, here's what it pushes out, and here's what I'd recommend." It's a no shaped as a decision they own.

**The story to have ready.** One real case, with the shape: a senior request you thought was wrong → the question you asked first → the alternative you proposed → what they chose → what happened. The strongest version is one where they **overruled you and you committed** — or one where your first question revealed that **you** were wrong. Both are more credible than one where you simply won.

**How they grade it:**

| Signal | Strong | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curiosity first | Asks what the request is solving before pushing back | Opens with "we don't have capacity" |
| Goal vs. solution | Keeps the goal, challenges the specified solution | Rejects the whole request |
| Options, not a veto | Offers the full cost, a cheaper version, and a recommendation | A flat no, or a silent yes |
| Concrete cost | Names what slips and who is affected | "My team is stretched" |
| Chain hygiene | Keeps their own manager informed; escalates jointly if at all | Goes around the person, or lets the manager be surprised |
| Commitment | Executes fully if overruled; no relitigating | Complies half-heartedly, or complains to the team |
| Self-awareness | Has a case where the senior person turned out to be right | Every story ends with them being proven right |

**Follow-ups to prepare:**

- "What if they didn't want options — they just said 'do it'?" (Then I do it, and I make sure the one sentence of cost is in writing. Pushing a second time without new information is the mistake.)
- "What if it was your manager's manager, and your manager agreed with them?" (Full answer in "When it's your skip-level, and your manager agrees" below.)
- "Have you ever been wrong about one of these?" (Have the example. It is the best evidence that your pushback is judgment, not resistance.)
- "How do you decide which requests are worth pushing back on at all?" (A rule: push back when the cost is large or irreversible, or when it breaks a commitment to someone else. Absorb the small ones — saying no too often spends the credibility you need for the big ones.)
- "What exactly did you say?" Rehearse your first sentence — the question — word for word.

## When it's your skip-level, and your manager agrees

The hardest version of this question: two levels above you agree, and you still think it's wrong. What's really being tested is whether you can disagree **without going around your manager**, and commit fully when you lose.

**1. Raise your bar for "I'm right."** When two people who see more of the company than you agree, the odds are good they know something you don't — a customer commitment, a board priority, a reorg, a budget constraint. Start from curiosity, not resistance.

**2. Talk to your manager first, privately.** Never go to the skip-level directly to argue against your manager's position — even if you're right, it damages the relationship with your manager, and the skip-level sees someone who goes around their boss. Ask: *"Help me understand why you're on board with this. What am I missing?"* Three things can happen:

- They give you context that changes your mind — commit, done.
- They share your concerns but chose to support it for strategic or political reasons — now you know the real constraint, and you can usually help meet it more cheaply.
- They haven't seen the cost you see — now you have something new to bring.

**3. Decide whether it's worth pushing at all.** Only if at least one is true: the cost is **large** (a quarter of the team, a broken commitment to another team), it's **hard to reverse** (architecture, a public commitment, a customer-facing change), or there's **real risk** (reliability, compliance, customer harm). Otherwise, say your piece once to your manager and commit. Saving pushback for the decisions that matter is what keeps it credible.

**4. If it's worth pushing, go with or through your manager.** Make the case once, in writing, and let your manager choose the channel:

> "I still think this costs us more than we're seeing. I wrote up the tradeoff — what it would take, what it pushes out, and a cheaper version that gets most of the outcome. Would you be open to raising it with [skip] together, or would you rather take it to them yourself?"

Your manager stays in control, the skip-level gets new information rather than a complaint, and the tradeoff is on record. The usual outcome is a compromise — smaller scope, a phased version, a later start.

**5. If it's still yes, commit fully.** Execute properly, don't reopen it without new information, and present it to your team as your decision — "we're doing this, and here's why it matters," not "leadership is making us."

**The one exception:** anything **ethical, legal, safety, or compliance**. Then escalating through proper channels is right even when it's uncomfortable — and you tell your manager first that you're doing it. One sentence on this is enough; it shows judgment without taking over the answer.

**The spoken answer:**

> If both my manager and their manager agree, my first assumption is that they know something I don't, so I start with my manager, privately: "Help me understand why you're on board — what am I missing?" Often that's the end of it.
>
> If I still think the cost is large or hard to reverse, I write up the tradeoff once — what it would take, what it pushes out, and a cheaper option — and I ask my manager how they want to raise it: together, or them carrying it. What I won't do is go to the skip-level around my manager. That breaks trust in both directions, even if I'm right.
>
> And if the answer is still yes, I commit fully, and my team hears it as my decision. The exception is anything ethical, legal, or safety-related — that I'd escalate regardless, but I'd tell my manager first that I'm doing it.

**A short example to adapt** (placeholder — replace with a real one):

> My skip-level wanted a dedicated model for a new market launch in six weeks, and my manager agreed. I thought it would pull two engineers off the platform migration three teams were depending on. In my 1:1 I learned the launch was a CEO-level commitment, which I hadn't known. So instead of arguing the whole thing, I proposed using the existing model with market-specific features — about 80% of the value, one engineer instead of two, and the migration slipped only two weeks. My manager took that to the skip-level, and they accepted it.

The best version shows that **your first question revealed context you didn't have.**

**How they grade the skip-level version:**

| Signal | Strong | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chain respect | Goes to their manager first, never around them | Escalates to the skip-level directly |
| Humility | Assumes missing context when two levels agree | Assumes both of them are wrong |
| Selectivity | Pushes only on large, irreversible, or risky costs | Pushes on everything, or nothing |
| Constructive | Brings options and a cheaper version, in writing | Just says "this is a bad idea" |
| Manager partnership | Lets the manager choose how to raise it | Puts the manager in an awkward spot |
| Commitment | Executes fully and owns it to the team | Complies grudgingly, or relitigates |
| Ethics line | Knows when escalating anyway is right | Never escalates, or always does |
