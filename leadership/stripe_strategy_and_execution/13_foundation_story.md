# Q13. Your foundation-over-firefighting story

[← Round 2 index](README.md)

This is your strongest judgment story and it answers at least four questions in this round: killing or reshaping a plan (Q13), roadmap process (Q10), pushing back on org priorities (Q12), and influence without authority. Learn it cold.

**The narrative:**

> During annual planning, several product teams each asked my team to build them a dedicated model for their own firefighting needs. Every request was reasonable on its own. But looking across them, the pattern was obvious: the models overlapped heavily, the same features would be rebuilt from scratch each time, and none of it would be reusable. Saying yes to all of it meant spending the year as a model factory — looking fast early, getting slower every quarter, and ending up with N systems nobody could maintain.
>
> The easy path was to accept all of it. Every stakeholder happy, good short-term optics, and I look like a team player. I pushed back on that plan instead and proposed the inverse: build the foundation first — shared feature pipelines and a reusable serving layer — then deliver the use cases on top of it in sequence.
>
> [HOW YOU SOLD IT: which use case you shipped first on the new foundation, what you promised the teams who had to wait, and how you got leadership to accept a slower-looking first quarter.]
>
> [THE OUTCOME, with numbers: time to build each subsequent model dropped from X to Y; N models now share the same pipeline; the foundation paid for itself by quarter Z. If the outcome was mixed, say that — honest beats perfect here.]
>
> [WHAT YOU'D DO DIFFERENTLY: e.g. started the conversation before planning rather than during it, picked a thinner first slice, set clearer expectations with the teams who waited.]

**Three things to add while you fill it in:**

1. **The sequencing detail.** The answer is much stronger if you did not make everyone wait for the whole platform — if you picked one real use case and built the foundation underneath it, so there was a shipped thing in the first quarter. If that's what happened, lead with it; it converts the story from "I bet the year on infrastructure" into "I found the version that de-risked itself."
2. **Who you made unhappy.** Name the team that waited and how they reacted. A pushback story with no cost sounds rehearsed.
3. **The evidence you used.** What made the overlap undeniable — did you map the requested features against each other and show the duplication? Showing the artifact you used to win the argument is far stronger than saying you argued.

**How they grade it:**

| Signal | Strong | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pattern recognition | Saw the overlap across separate requests | Treated each request in isolation |
| Courage | Turned down the easy, popular path | Framed as obvious, with no real cost |
| Evidence | Shows the artifact that made the case — the overlap map | "I convinced them" |
| Sequencing | Shipped a real use case on the foundation early | Asked everyone to wait for the whole platform |
| Cost acknowledged | Names who waited and how they reacted | Everyone was happy |
| Measured outcome | Before/after numbers on time to build the next model | "It went really well" |
| Reflection | A specific thing they would do differently | Nothing to change |

**Follow-ups to prepare — these will all come:**

- "Why not just hire more people and do both?"
- "The teams who had to wait — how did they react, and what exactly did you tell them?"
- "What if the foundation had taken twice as long? What was your plan B?"
- "How did you measure whether the foundation was actually worth it?"
- "How did you know you weren't just building the platform you found more interesting?" (The honest answer names the discipline you imposed on yourself — the first use case shipping on it, a scope you refused to build, a deadline you held yourself to.)
