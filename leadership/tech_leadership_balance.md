# Technical + Leadership Balance: Operating as Tech Lead Manager

## Your Unique Position

You're not a pure manager and not a pure IC. You're a **tech lead manager**—someone who:
- Makes technical decisions and owns architecture
- Manages people and builds team capabilities
- Stays hands-on without being a bottleneck
- Has credibility in both domains

This is a competitive advantage, not a weakness. Own it.

---

## Why This Matters for Roblox

Critical harm is a complex domain that needs:
1. **Technical depth** — ML models, systems thinking, scalability
2. **Leadership capability** — team building, cross-functional alignment, decision-making
3. **Hands-on judgment** — you need enough technical credibility to catch problems early

A manager who can code and understand ML is rare and valuable. A pure manager might miss technical risks; a pure IC might not scale thinking from systems to teams.

## How to Operate Effectively

### Time Allocation (as a Tech Lead Manager of ~10 engineers)
- **60-70%**: People leadership, strategy, cross-functional alignment
  - 1:1s (10 engineers × 1 hr/week = 10 hrs)
  - Hiring/planning (5 hrs/week)
  - Meetings with other teams, leadership (8 hrs/week)
  - Mentoring, feedback, career development (3 hrs/week)

- **20-30%**: Technical leadership
  - Design reviews (2-3 hrs/week)
  - Code review/feedback (2 hrs/week)
  - Technical spike/investigation (2-3 hrs/week)
  - Architecture decisions (1-2 hrs/week)

- **10-15%**: Hands-on coding (when needed)
  - Critical bug fix, technical spike, unblocking work
  - Not daily—opportunistic and intentional

### Decision Rights
**You own:**
- Team vision and strategy
- Hiring and people decisions
- Technical architecture and design
- Prioritization and roadmap

**Engineers own:**
- Day-to-day implementation
- Code quality and design reviews (with your oversight)
- Technical problem-solving within agreed direction
- Their own career development (with your guidance)

**You delegate to senior ICs:**
- Code review of major features
- Mentoring junior engineers
- Technical leadership on specific systems
- Investigation of technical issues

### How to Stay Credible

**Do:**
- Spend time understanding the systems (read code, run it locally)
- Participate in design reviews with technical depth (ask hard questions)
- Code occasionally on important problems (architecture exploration, spike)
- Stay on-call or shadow on-call (understand production)
- Keep learning (new tools, papers, frameworks)

**Don't:**
- Try to code 50% of the work (you'll be mediocre at both jobs)
- Make technical decisions in a vacuum (always ask the team)
- Review every PR (that's not a sign of rigor, it's a sign you're a bottleneck)
- Code for visibility (do it because the work needs doing)

## Real Example: Your Fraud Detection Work

### How you likely operate today:
- **Monday planning**: Decide with team what to build this sprint (strategy)
- **Tuesday design review**: New fraud model approach, you ask tough questions about assumptions and tradeoffs (technical leadership)
- **Wednesday-Thursday**: Code a spike on improving feature engineering or investigate a production issue (hands-on)
- **Friday**: 1:1s with engineers, discuss career growth, give feedback (people leadership)

This is healthy. You're in the room for big decisions but not writing most of the code.

### Mistakes to avoid:
- ❌ Trying to review all code and design all features (bottleneck)
- ❌ Coding so much that you miss team signals (out of touch)
- ❌ Never coding and relying on past knowledge (lose credibility)

## How to Talk About This in Interviews

### When asked "What's your role as tech lead manager?"
**Answer:**
"I'm responsible for team strategy, hiring, and people development—but I stay hands-on technically. I spend about 20-30% of my time on technical leadership: leading design reviews, investigating hard problems, mentoring senior engineers. I also code occasionally on important work—maybe 10-15% of my time—to stay sharp and unblock the team.

The balance matters. If I only managed, I'd lose technical credibility and miss problems early. If I only coded, I wouldn't scale my impact to the team. The sweet spot is using my technical judgment to set direction while growing the team to own the execution."

### When asked "Aren't you stretched thin?"
**Answer:**
"I think the opposite. A strong technical foundation makes me a better manager. I understand the problems my team is solving, so I can help them navigate tradeoffs and prioritize effectively. And having managed people makes me a better engineer—I think about how decisions affect the team.

The potential trap is trying to do both at 100%. I don't. I prioritize people when there's a conflict (someone struggling, hiring, etc.), but I protect time for technical work because it makes me effective in both roles.

And critically: I hire senior engineers who can own technical work without my input. My job is to set direction and grow them, not to be the linchpin."

### When asked about scaling
**Answer:**
"As the team grows from 10 to 15-20 engineers, my ratio of hands-on work will shift further toward leadership. I'll hire or promote a technical lead IC to own more of the architecture work. But I'll never be purely a manager—I'll always want to stay credible enough to have sound technical judgment about our systems.

That's how I see leadership in a technical domain: you grow the team to be less dependent on you, but you stay credible enough to guide them well."

## The Downside (Be Honest)

This role is genuinely hard:
- You're context-switching all day (person → technical problem → person)
- You're not an expert in either domain at the deepest level
- It's easy to feel like you're doing both roles poorly sometimes
- Some "pure" managers might scale faster; some "pure" ICs might code faster

But for a team of 10-20 people with complex technical problems:
- Technical leadership from someone who manages = better decisions
- Management from someone who codes = better execution
- It's a rare combo, and it's valuable
