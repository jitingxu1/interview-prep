## Answer structure (for "a time you were the bottleneck / a mistake you made")

| Step | What to say |
|---|---|
| **1. Own it** | "I was the bottleneck," in the first sentence |
| **2. Situation** | The scope and why it was more than one person could hold |
| **3. Cost** | How you noticed, with a number if you have one |
| **4. Root cause** | Your own habit, not "I didn't have time" |
| **5. What you changed** | The mechanism: owners, written decision rights, gradual handoff |
| **6. Result** | Numbers, people who grew, what it freed you to do |
| **7. Close** | The drag you still watch, and how you track it |

## Final answer (~2 minutes)

> When I started managing the fraud ML team, I was the bottleneck, and it took me too long to see it.
>
> The team had four areas, and each had its own stakeholders: a product manager, data scientists, data and backend engineers, and business partners. Every area had a weekly sync, and I was in all four, plus every decision that came out of them.
>
> I noticed it in three ways. Decisions waited on me while I switched between areas. My calendar was every area's stakeholder calendar. And the engineers closest to each problem, who had the most context, were bringing their decisions to me instead of making them. **[ADD A REAL NUMBER: decisions waiting on you each week, or meetings per week.]**
>
> The problem wasn't my time, it was the design. I came up as a tech lead, and my instinct was: if something matters, I own it. That worked for one team. It didn't work for four areas.
>
> So I made three changes. Each area got an owner, usually the senior engineer with the most context. I wrote down the decision rights: inside their area, they decide and tell me after. Anything that crosses areas, touches another team's metrics, is expensive to reverse, or changes headcount comes to me. And I handed over gradually: I co-ran their syncs for a few weeks, then sat in while they ran them, then stepped out. Now I read the sync notes and join when they need me, plus a weekly owners' sync where cross-area issues come up.
>
> That freed me to work on the whole team: the roadmap, hiring, and how the areas fit together. **[ADD REAL RESULTS: decisions to you from X to Y a week; owners who grew into bigger scope; stakeholder feedback.]**
>
> It's not fully solved. On high-stakes calls my instinct is still to step in early. But now I catch it, and I track it by how many decisions still come to me each week.

**Why this lands:** you own it in the first sentence, name the cost, find the root cause in your own habit, show a real mechanism (owners, written decision rights, a gradual handoff), and close with the drag you still watch. For a coaching-heavy interviewer like Rula's Jay Floyd, this is his "find the leadership drag" and "rebuild delegation and ownership" steps, told as your story.

## Likely follow-ups

Full answers for these are in [weaknesses.md](./weaknesses.md#follow-ups):

- *Why did it take so long to notice?* Things were getting done, just through me. "It's still getting done" is a lagging signal; now I watch decision wait time and my calendar.
- *How did you choose the owners?* **[NEEDS YOUR STORY: real criteria, e.g. context on the area, judgment, credibility with that area's stakeholders.]**
- *What happened when an owner made a wrong call?* Inside their area and reversible: they own fixing it, and we talk about it in 1:1. Irreversible or cross-area calls come to me by design.
- *Did stakeholders push back on not having you in the room?* **[NEEDS YOUR STORY]** The gradual handoff was for exactly this.
- *When did you last slip back?* **[NEEDS YOUR STORY: a real recent case where you jumped into a delegated decision, and how you caught it.]**
- *How would this scale to a bigger team?* Same model one level up: owners become TLs or managers, with decision rights written the same way.
