## Final answer (~90 seconds)

> Visibility is two different things: where you share, and how you say it. The second one matters more.
>
> **How I say it:** I report in business terms, not technical ones. The first line is the decision or the ask, the second is the business impact (dollars, customers, risk), and the technical detail goes in the appendix. The clearest example was a fraud-threshold decision. Instead of debating precision and recall, I put fraud dollars prevented and legitimate customers blocked on the same chart, at each threshold. The room argued about a number instead of a principle, and we agreed on a phased rollout in one meeting.
>
> **Who says it:** I have the engineers present their own work, at all-hands demos and in our tech forum. Leadership sees the people, not just the project, and it builds the case for their growth long before a promotion conversation.
>
> **Where:** a weekly newsletter for the whole org, a shared project tracker so anyone can see status, and trainings and Q&A sessions with partner teams.
>
> **Does it land:** I check by asking leaders what they remember, and by watching whether other teams start coming to us early. If the work is strong but the message isn't landing, that's my problem to fix, not the team's.

**[CONFIRM: be ready to say roughly what the fraud thresholds and trade-offs were, in case you're asked.]**

**Why this lands:**
- It answers both halves of the question: the channels *and* the translation.
- A real example with a result (one meeting, phased rollout).
- Engineers presenting links visibility to growth and promotions ([leveling_and_promotions.md](./leveling_and_promotions.md)).
- You own the outcome. For Rula's Jay Floyd, this matches his "become legible to leadership" and "stop over-explaining, start being understood."

## Likely follow-ups

### Quick hints

| Follow-up | Hint | Key line |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership doesn't value tech debt or reliability? | **Translate into risk and cost** | "What does it cost us if we don't?" |
| Your team's work shows up in someone else's project? | **Name the people, publicly and specifically** | "Credit is cheap to give and expensive to lose." |
| Quiet engineers' work? | **Evidence, a stage that suits them, speak for them** | "My job is to make their work visible, not to make them louder." |

### 1. What do you do when leadership doesn't value work that matters, like tech debt or reliability?

> I stop calling it tech debt and start talking about what it costs. Leadership doesn't fund "refactoring"; it funds lower risk and faster delivery. So I put numbers on it: how many incidents it caused last quarter, how many engineer hours we lose to it every week, how much slower new features ship because of it. Then I ask the real question: what does it cost us if we don't fix it? I also make it smaller: not "a quarter of tech debt," but "two weeks to remove the cause of our top incident." And I tie it to something they already care about, like a launch that depends on it. When the cost is visible, it usually gets prioritised.

**[OPTIONAL STORY: a real time you got reliability or tech-debt work funded by showing its cost.]**

### 2. How do you handle credit when your team's work shows up in someone else's project?

> I make sure the people get named, publicly and specifically. Not "my team helped," but "Priya built the feature pipeline that made this launch possible." I say it in the meeting, in the newsletter, and in writing to their manager or the project lead. I'm not trying to take credit away from the other team; shared wins are good. But my engineers' work needs to be visible to the people who will review them. Credit is cheap to give and expensive to lose: if people feel invisible, they stop going the extra mile.

(Use a real name only if you mean a real person; the name above is an example.)

### 3. How do you make the work of quiet engineers visible?

> I judge on evidence, not airtime: design docs, code reviews, outcomes, and what their peers say about working with them. Quiet people often do the work that holds everything together, like reviews, mentoring, and fixing what nobody else wants to touch, so I make that work visible. I give them a stage that suits them: a written design doc or a small-group demo, not necessarily a big meeting. And in calibration, I'm their advocate: I bring the specific evidence, so their case doesn't depend on whether other managers have heard them speak. My job is to make their work visible, not to make them louder.
