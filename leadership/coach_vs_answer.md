## Final answer (~90 seconds)

> Coaching is my default, but I don't coach everything. I decide by asking three quick questions.
>
> **First, how urgent and high-stakes is it?** If production is down or a customer is affected, I give the answer, clearly, and we talk about the why afterward. Coaching in the middle of an incident just costs time.
>
> **Second, does the person already have what they need to find it?** If they do, and a wrong turn is cheap, I coach. I ask, "What do you think?" or "What have you tried?" That's where people grow and take ownership.
>
> **Third, what's in between?** When there's a real trade-off, I give bounded room: "Take two days to explore it, then bring me what you've got." They still do the thinking, but it can't drift.
>
> And one rule: if someone has asked me twice, I stop coaching and just help. At that point holding back isn't teaching, it's frustrating.
>
> The warning sign I watch for is dependence. If someone needs me to rebuild their confidence every week, I'm over-coaching. The goal is that they walk out of the conversation with their own reasons to move, not mine.
>
> **[EXAMPLE, ONE SENTENCE: e.g. "Recently a junior engineer asked how to set up the evaluation for a new model. Instead of answering, I asked what metric the business actually cares about. He came back with a better setup than I'd have suggested."]**

## Likely follow-ups

### Quick hints

| Follow-up | Hint | Key line |
|---|---|---|
| Coaching is too slow, deadline is close? | **Answer now, debrief after** | "The learning can happen after the deadline." |
| Someone just wants the answer? | **Ask what help they want** | "Sometimes the most useful question is 'what kind of help do you want?'" |
| A time coaching didn't work? | **Noticed → why (my call) → switched → lesson** | "Coaching only works when the person has something to build on." |

### 1. What if coaching is too slow and the deadline is close?

> Then I give the answer, and the learning happens afterward. A short debrief once the deadline has passed: "Here's why I went that way. What would you have done?" That way the deadline is met, and they still get the reasoning. What I avoid is coaching under pressure: it stresses them and slows everyone down.

### 2. How do you coach someone who just wants the answer?

> I ask what kind of help they want. Sometimes people just need the answer to get unblocked, and that's fine. If it's something they'll face again, I'll say, "I'll tell you, but first give me your best guess." It takes thirty seconds and they still do some of the thinking. If someone always wants the answer, that's a confidence signal, and I work on that directly.

### 3. Give me an example where you coached and it didn't work.

**What the interviewer is testing:** not that coaching failed once, but that you **noticed** quickly, **worked out why** (and owned it), and **switched modes**, with a rule you use now. It's weak if it sounds like you blame the other person.

**Framework: noticed → why → switched → lesson**

| Step | What to say | ~Time |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Situation** | Who, what problem, why you chose to coach | 15s |
| **2. The signal** | How you saw it wasn't working | 10s |
| **3. Why it failed** | Your diagnosis, owned by you | 15s |
| **4. What you switched to** | More direction, a worked example, pairing | 15s |
| **5. Result** | What happened after the switch | 10s |
| **6. Lesson** | The rule you use now | 10s |

**Common reasons coaching fails** (use these to find your real story):

| Why coaching failed | What it looked like | What works instead |
|---|---|---|
| **They didn't have the base skill yet** | You asked "what do you think?" and they had no idea where to start | Show a worked example first, then coach the next one |
| **The goal wasn't clear** | They worked hard in the wrong direction | Define "good" up front, then coach on how to get there |
| **It was too urgent** | A deadline slipped while they explored | Give the answer now, and debrief afterward |
| **Low confidence, not low skill** | They had good ideas but kept asking you to confirm | More encouragement and a clear "it's your call" |
| **They wanted to be told** | They got frustrated by all the questions | Ask them what kind of help they want |
| **Too many open questions at once** | They felt lost, not empowered | Narrow it: one decision at a time |

The first one is the most common, and the best fit for you: it ties straight back to the leadership-style answer (the skill on *this specific problem* was low, so coaching was the wrong mode).

**Questions to find your real story:**
- Was there a time you asked "what do you think?" and got silence, or a wrong direction, several times in a row?
- Did a junior engineer ever take much longer than expected because you held back?
- Did anyone ever say, "Can you just tell me what you want?"
- Did a deadline ever slip because you let someone explore too long?

**Template answer (fill in the brackets with your real story):**

> One time it didn't work was with **[a junior engineer]** on **[task, e.g. designing the evaluation for a new fraud model]**. I wanted them to own it, so I coached: I asked questions instead of giving the approach.
>
> After **[a week / two 1:1s]**, I could see it wasn't working: **[they kept coming back with the same question / they went deep in the wrong direction / they looked stuck and stressed]**.
>
> When I stepped back, I realised it was my call that was wrong, not their effort. **[They'd never designed an evaluation like this before]**, so asking "what do you think?" didn't help. There was nothing for them to build on yet.
>
> So I switched. **[I walked through one example with them end to end / we paired for an hour / I gave them a clear template]**, then let them do the next part on their own.
>
> **[Result: e.g. they finished it in a few days, and on the next model they designed the evaluation themselves.]**
>
> The lesson I took is that coaching only works when the person has something to build on. Now, if the skill is new to them, I show one first, then coach.
