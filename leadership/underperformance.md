## Final answer (~90 seconds)

> My rule is that a performance review should never be the first time someone hears they're behind. So I catch it early, diagnose before I judge, and agree on a clear plan.
>
> **Early:** I watch for patterns, not one bad week: missed commitments, quality slipping, someone going quiet. When I see a pattern, I raise it in our next 1:1, not at review time.
>
> **Diagnose:** I ask three questions, in order. Clarity: do they actually know what good looks like? Often it's a communication problem, not a capability one. Fit: are they in a role that plays to their strengths? Support: have I given them the time, tools, and help they need? And sometimes it's something personal, so I ask.
>
> **Agree a plan:** I tell them what I see, what good looks like, and I ask for their view. If we see it differently, that's the conversation. Then we write down a plan with specific goals, a timeline, and weekly check-ins.
>
> **Example:** I had a backend engineer who was struggling on his team. The easy path was a performance plan, but the diagnosis said fit, not effort: he was strong in infrastructure, just in the wrong seat. We moved him to the infrastructure side, and he became core to our platform work.
>
> **If it doesn't improve:** if the plan runs its course and the gap is still there, we have an honest conversation about fit, and I bring in HR if it needs to become formal. The goal is that whatever happens, whether they improve, move, or leave, nobody is surprised and it's fair.

**Why this lands:**
- It's a clear system, not good intentions: early → diagnose → plan → escalate.
- The two-way conversation shows respect.
- A real example where the diagnosis changed the outcome.
- The full path is covered, so the interviewer doesn't wonder what happens if the plan fails.

For a coaching-heavy interviewer like Rula's Jay Floyd, this is his "strengthen performance and hard conversations" step: structure and timing, and nobody surprised at review time.

**Consistency check:** you've never managed anyone out (say so plainly if asked; see [weaknesses.md](./weaknesses.md) and the Rula Q11). So don't imply you've run a formal PIP. "I bring in HR if it needs to become formal" describes how you *would* handle it.

### 1. How long do you give someone before it becomes formal?

> It depends on the gap, but I'm explicit about it up front. Usually it's four to eight weeks of the written plan, with weekly check-ins, long enough to show a real trend but not so long that the team carries the gap for a quarter. What matters most is that the person knows the timeline from day one.

### 2. What if they disagree with your assessment?

> Then the disagreement is the conversation. I show specific examples, not general impressions, and I ask what they see that I don't. Sometimes they're right: I'm missing context, or the expectation was never clear, which is on me. If we still disagree, we agree on a measurable goal for the next few weeks and let the result settle it.

**Going deeper (if they probe):**

*Why people disagree, and what each one needs.* Find out which it is before you respond:

| What's behind it | What it sounds like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **The expectation was never clear** | "Nobody told me that was the bar." | Often true. Own it, write down the expectation now, and reset the clock. |
| **You're missing context** | "That project was blocked by another team for three weeks." | Check it. If they're right, change your assessment and say so. |
| **They compare themselves to the wrong level** | "I'm doing the same as [peer]." | Show the level expectations side by side; talk about their work, not the peer's. |
| **They see the facts but not the impact** | "Yes, it was late, but it wasn't a big deal." | Connect it to the impact: who waited, what slipped, what it cost. |
| **Defensiveness: it just hurts to hear** | Pushback on everything, emotional | Slow down. Don't argue in the moment; offer to continue tomorrow once they've had time to think. |

*How I run the conversation:*
1. **Specific examples, not labels.** Not "your work is sloppy," but "the last two design docs went to review without the rollback plan."
2. **Ask before defending.** "What do you see that I'm not seeing?" Then actually listen; don't wait for your turn.
3. **Separate the facts from the judgment.** Agree on what happened first. Disagreeing about what it *means* is easier once the facts are shared.
4. **Change your mind out loud when they're right.** "You're right, I didn't know that. That changes my view on X." It builds trust, and it makes the rest of the feedback land.
5. **If you still disagree, agree on a test.** "Let's agree on what good looks like for the next four weeks: these two deliverables, at this quality. If you hit it, I was wrong." A measurable goal ends the argument without anyone having to give in.
6. **Write it down.** Send a short summary after the 1:1: what we agreed, what we still see differently, the goal, and the next check-in. It prevents a second disagreement about what was said.
7. **Offer a second opinion if it stays stuck.** A skip-level or a peer reviewer can look at the same work. It shows you're confident and fair, not just asserting authority.

*Phrases that help:*
- "I might be missing something. Walk me through how you see it."
- "Let's start with what we agree on."
- "I hear that you see it differently. Here's the specific thing I'm looking at."
- "We don't have to agree today. Let's agree on how we'll know."

*Traps to avoid:*
- **Winning the argument.** If you "win" and they leave unconvinced, nothing changes.
- **Backing down to avoid the discomfort.** That moves the surprise to review time, which is exactly what you're trying to prevent.
- **Bringing up everything at once.** Two or three specific points, not a list of every miss.
- **Comparing them to a named peer.** It turns a performance conversation into a fairness fight.

### 3. What if the cause is personal: burnout, family, health?

> Then performance comes second for a while. I ask what would help: lighter scope, time off, flexible hours. I bring in HR for anything formal like leave, and I protect their privacy with the team. Once things are steadier, we go back to the plan. People remember how you treated them in a hard moment.

### 4. How do you keep it from affecting the rest of the team?

> I keep it private: the conversations happen 1:1, and the team doesn't hear about someone's plan from me. But I also don't let the gap fall silently on others. If someone is covering extra work, I see it, I rebalance, and I thank them. Teams notice whether a manager deals with performance or ignores it, and ignoring it hurts the high performers most.

**Going deeper (if they probe):**

*How underperformance spreads to the team:*

| Effect | What it looks like | What I do |
|---|---|---|
| **Extra load on others** | Teammates quietly fix their PRs, finish their tasks, cover on-call | Notice it, rebalance the work explicitly, and thank the people covering, in private and, where appropriate, in public |
| **Blocked dependencies** | Others wait on their piece of the project | Re-plan: smaller scope for that person, or move the critical-path piece to someone else while they work on the plan |
| **The bar drifts down** | "If they can ship that, why can't I?" | Hold the standard in reviews for everyone, consistently. The team needs to see the bar is the same |
| **Resentment and gossip** | Side conversations, frustration in retros | Don't discuss the person; say "I'm aware and I'm handling it" to whoever raises it |
| **High performers start to leave** | Your best people disengage or interview elsewhere | Ask them directly in 1:1s how the load feels; this is the most expensive effect, and it's silent |

*How I handle it, step by step:*
1. **Privacy first.** The plan, the conversations, and the details stay between me, the person, and HR. Nobody on the team should learn about someone's plan from me.
2. **Re-plan the work.** Take the critical-path work off them for now. Give them scope they can succeed on and that shows clearly whether they're improving.
3. **Rebalance openly, without saying why.** "I'm moving X to you because it's on the critical path" is enough. Don't say "because they're struggling."
4. **Recognise the people covering.** Thank them in 1:1s, and make sure the extra work shows up in their reviews. Covering for someone shouldn't be invisible.
5. **Answer concerns without gossip.** If someone raises it: "Thanks for telling me. I'm aware, and I'm working on it. I can't share details, the same way I wouldn't share yours." That builds trust both ways.
6. **Keep the bar visible for everyone.** The same standards in code review and design review, for everyone. The team watches whether the bar applies to all.
7. **Set a time limit.** The team can carry a gap for a few weeks, not a quarter. That's another reason the plan needs a clear timeline.

*Phrases that help:*
- To the person covering: "I've noticed you've picked up a lot of X these last few weeks. Thank you. I'm making sure it's recognised, and I'm working on the underlying issue."
- To someone who raises it: "I'm aware and I'm on it. I can't go into details, but I won't let it drag on."
- To the team, when moving work: "I'm shifting X to [name] because it's on the critical path."

*Traps to avoid:*
- **Silent overload.** Letting high performers absorb the gap without saying anything. They notice, and they leave.
- **Venting to the team.** Even a small comment destroys trust: everyone wonders what you say about them.
- **Lowering the bar for one person.** It feels kind, but the team sees it as unfair.
- **Waiting too long.** The longer it drags on, the more the team pays, and the more they question your judgment.

### 5. Have you ever had someone not improve?

> Not to the point of managing someone out. The closest was the backend engineer: the fit check changed the answer, and he thrived in a different role. If it ever comes to that, I'd want it to follow the same honest sequence, with expectations in writing and no surprises.

### 6. What if a previously strong performer starts slipping?

> I treat that differently from someone who's never met the bar. Something changed, so I ask what: a new project they don't connect with, a personal situation, burnout, or something on the team. Usually the fix is a conversation, not a plan.
