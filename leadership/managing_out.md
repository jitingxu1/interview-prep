# "Have you ever had to manage someone out?"

Your honest answer is **no**. Say so plainly (the rule from [weaknesses.md](./weaknesses.md)), then show you'd do it well and won't avoid it. Built Nov 24. Company-specific version: [Rula / Jay Floyd Q11](../company/rula/jay-floyd-leadership-qa.md). Related: [underperformance.md](./underperformance.md) (the full process, and the home of the backend-engineer story).

---

## What the interviewer is testing

- **Honesty:** will you say "no" plainly, or dress up a story you don't have?
- **Willingness:** will you actually do it when needed, or avoid it?
- **Process:** would you handle it fairly, with no surprises and with dignity?

---

## Framework: honest no → closest case → how I'd do it → what I believe

| Step | What to say | ~Time |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Honest no** | "No, and I don't want to dress something up." | 5s |
| **2. Closest case** | The backend engineer, in one line | 10s |
| **3. How I'd do it** | Clarity → fit → support checked first; a written, time-bound plan; HR early; no surprises; dignity | 30s |
| **4. What I believe** | Willing to do it; it's fair to the team; the last step, not the first | 10s |

---

## Final answer (~60 seconds)

> No, I haven't, and I'd rather be straight about that than dress something up.
>
> The closest I've come is the backend engineer I mentioned: the fit check changed the outcome, and he thrived in a different role.
>
> If I do have to, I'd follow the same honest sequence. First, make sure I've really checked clarity, fit, and support. Then a written plan with specific goals and a timeline, with HR involved early. Every conversation would be direct, so the final decision is never a surprise. And if it comes to that, I'd handle the exit with dignity: a respectful conversation, help with the transition, and a short, respectful message to the team without details.
>
> I don't see it as a failure to avoid at all costs. Keeping someone in a role they can't succeed in isn't kind to them, and it isn't fair to the team carrying the gap. It's the last step, not the first, but I won't avoid it when it's the right call.

**Why it works:** an honest "no" up front; one line pointing to the backend-engineer story instead of retelling it; a clear process; and a close that answers the hidden worry (that you'd avoid it).

---

## Traps

- **Dressing up a non-story.** The interviewer will dig, and it'll fall apart.
- **"I'd never manage someone out" / "I always find another role."** Sounds like you avoid hard calls.
- **Sounding cold:** "I'd just put them on a PIP." No empathy, no process.
- **Implying you've run a formal PIP** when you haven't.

---

## Quick hint

> **Honest no → backend engineer (one line) → clarity/fit/support → written plan + HR → no surprises → dignity → won't avoid it.**
> Key line: *"Keeping someone in a role they can't succeed in isn't kind to them, and it isn't fair to the team."*

## Likely follow-ups

| Follow-up | Hint | Key line |
|---|---|---|
| Would you be able to do it? | **Yes, and why** | "Avoiding it is the unkind option. The team pays for it." |
| How would you tell the team? | **Short, respectful, no details, focus on what's next** | "They'll judge me on how I treat the person who left." |
| What if the person is well-liked? | **Same bar for everyone; be kind, not unclear** | "Being liked and meeting the bar are different things, and both matter." |
| Have you seen it done badly? | **A surprise exit, and what it did to trust** | "Nobody should be surprised by their own exit." **[Only if you've actually seen one]** |
