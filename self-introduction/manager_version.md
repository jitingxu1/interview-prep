# Self-Introduction: Manager Version

**Emphasize: Leadership, team building, people development, organizational impact**

---

I'm Jiting, and I'm currently a tech lead and manager at DoorDash in our opex org. I'm leading the ML and AI team—we've got 12 engineers split across a couple of pods focused on customer support automation, fraud detection, and abuse prevention.

One thing I'm really proud of is that I built this team completely from scratch. My role covers a lot—I'm thinking about the long-term technical vision, the planning and roadmaps, hitting milestones, training and growing the junior engineers, handling cross-functional alignment with product and business, and then also the hiring and coaching. So it's really about making sure we have the right people, they're growing, we're hitting our milestones, and we're executing on a solid technical vision.

As a manager, I do regular one-on-ones where we talk about career goals, pain points, and how I can support them. I've helped multiple engineers level up—identifying high performers early and creating growth plans for them. I focus on developing them as well-rounded engineers: technical skills, people skills, and product and project management experience. I also really believe in clarity: clear expectations, clear feedback, clear career paths. That's what helps people thrive.

On the org side, I'm responsible for planning our roadmap, making sure we're aligned with product and the business, and handling cross-functional communication. A lot of my day is spent being a bridge between my engineers and the rest of the organization—translating between technical and business realities.

But here's the thing: I don't think good managers stop doing technical work. I'm still very hands-on. I review major technical decisions, I stay involved with architecture, I can debug hard problems. I think that credibility—actually understanding what your team is working on—is essential. Engineers respect managers who can still do the work.

Before DoorDash, I worked at VoltronData and claypot as an engineering manager. There I led teams working on Ibis and Ibis-ML—open source projects that are getting real adoption in the data and ML communities. Ibis is like pandas but scalable—you write code once and run it across 20+ different query engines. Ibis-ML is similar to scikit-learn for preprocessing, but scalable as well. Managing open source teams taught me a lot about distributed teams, asynchronous communication, and motivating people who are working on things they're genuinely passionate about.

Before that, at Airbnb, I was a tech lead. I built the location graph—everything from data collection to ranking and understanding. I also worked on user engagement, figuring out how to send the right content to the right users at the right time. That's where I first started thinking about scaling—not just scaling systems, but scaling teams and making technical decisions that a whole org depends on. I learned how to lead without formal authority, how to influence through credibility and good judgment.

The common thread through all of this is: I care about building strong teams. I care about developing people. And I think the best managers are still deeply technical—they're not just managing people, they're also stewarding the technical vision and helping their teams do their best work.
